Bank of England holds rates as inflation stalls above target

Policymakers split 5-4 on whether to resume cuts, with the governor warning that services inflation remains “stubbornly sticky” heading into the winter.

Fedor Testing in London · 2 October 2026 · 6 min read
Share on X LinkedIn Email Save

The Bank of England left interest rates unchanged on Thursday, pausing a cycle of cuts that began last year, after its rate-setting committee split almost down the middle over whether price pressures had eased enough to justify further loosening.

The Monetary Policy Committee voted 5-4 to hold the bank rate at 4 per cent, with the four dissenters favouring a quarter-point reduction. The decision had been widely expected by markets, but the narrowness of the vote surprised some economists who had anticipated a more decisive hold.

Headline inflation has drifted sideways at just under 3 per cent for three consecutive months, above the central bank’s 2 per cent target. Services inflation, which policymakers watch closely as a gauge of domestic price pressures, remains above 4.5 per cent.

“We are not yet confident that the disinflation process has run its course. Patience is a policy choice, not an absence of one.”

A divided committee

In a press conference following the decision, the governor said the committee had “genuine and legitimate differences of view” about the balance of risks. Those favouring a cut pointed to a softening labour market, with vacancies falling to their lowest level since 2021 and wage growth decelerating in the private sector.

Those preferring to hold cited persistent services inflation, upward revisions to the near-term growth forecast, and the risk that fiscal measures expected in the Budget later this month could add to demand.

Sterling rose modestly against the dollar after the announcement, while two-year gilt yields climbed four basis points as traders trimmed bets on a cut at the next meeting in December.

What it means for borrowers

Mortgage lenders had already priced in a hold, and most analysts do not expect significant moves in fixed-rate products in the short term. Variable-rate borrowers will see no immediate change to their monthly payments.

“The direction of travel is still towards lower rates,” said one economist at a large UK bank. “But the pace is going to be slower than the market was hoping for at the start of the year, and the committee has made clear it will not be rushed.”

The next decision is due on 18 December. Markets currently assign roughly a 55 per cent probability to a quarter-point cut at that meeting.
