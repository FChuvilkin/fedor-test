China's tribute system and the new world order

Exercising power without resorting to war could be Beijing's ultimate show of strength

For roughly 2,000 years, China ran its foreign relations through what historians call the tribute system. Neighbouring states acknowledged the emperor's pre-eminence, sent gifts, and in return received trade access, protection and legitimacy. China rarely needed to conquer. It needed only to be the place everyone had to deal with.

I have spent the past year studying this history because I believe it explains how Beijing thinks about power today far better than the western frame of empire and alliance. Chinese leaders do not seek to build empires to control other countries because they view doing so as ineffective and expensive. They seek to make China indispensable.

Look at the pattern. Trade agreements that tie supply chains to Chinese manufacturing. Infrastructure finance that makes Chinese banks the lender of record across the global south. A currency that is slowly becoming the settlement medium for commodities. None of these are acts of war. All of them shift the centre of gravity.

The US, by contrast, has spent the past decade oscillating between coercion and withdrawal. Tariffs raise the cost of dealing with America. Sanctions make the dollar system something to route around. Each move is defensible on its own terms, and each pushes countries towards the alternative that Beijing is quietly building.

This matters for investors because the big cycles I study, the debt cycle, the internal order cycle and the external order cycle, are all turning at once. When a rising power can offer the world's middle powers a better deal than the incumbent, the transition tends to happen through trade and finance long before it happens through force.

Exercising power without resorting to war could be Beijing's ultimate show of strength. The question for Washington is whether it can rediscover how to be the partner everyone wants, rather than the partner everyone fears.
